This is the tradeoff from the opening chart, said directly. It is the one central tradeoff of the talk.

  • A big language, like seaborn, covers many charts with short specs, but it needs a new function for every new chart.
  • A small language, like d3, stays small, but every spec is long.
  • Steele says not to pick either one. The middle path is a hierarchy of abstractions: a small core, and new levels built from the ones below it. The small core keeps the language small, and the levels give the big vocabulary that short specs need.

a language that grows is a hierarchy

This slide is the answer to the first objection.

  • Open with the callback: "Remember the cliff on the compression chart. The grammar of graphics is excellent until the cliff, and past it the only move is to add a new noun. That is the left half: one flat layer of nouns, with every chart written directly against it."
  • On the right, the same charts sit on intermediate words (chart, tree layout, diagram), and those words are built from a small core of marks and verbs like spread, stack, and relate.
  • The middle blocks are domain languages, Andrew's small modular components. Chart, tree layout, and diagram each have their own words, fit to their own domain.
  • Extensibility is what keeps the hierarchy together. Each new DSL is built from the same core with the same verbs, from inside the language. So the DSLs do not fall apart into separate islands, the way the grammars in the tangle did. Each domain gets its own language, with its own design, and the whole is still one language.
  • Say the full thesis: "In the traditional grammar of graphics, the only way to grow the language is to add a new primitive. In a language with verbs, you can build new structures from old ones, using a fairly stable set of composition mechanisms."
  • The core stays small while the vocabulary gets big. That is the sweet spot on the compression chart.
  • The faded block in the middle is the point: the middle level is open, not a closed set of three kinds. New DSLs can keep being added to it.
  • The next slides show one of those DSLs, GoTree, which happens to be for tree data.

The leaves are charts the audience has already seen. The population pyramid, GoTree silhouette tree, Sankey tree, and Bluefish pulley diagram are on the splash wall. The ribbon chart is from the crash course.

Lead into GoTree: "Here is one part of that hierarchy, up close. It happens to be a language for tree data."

If asked about "optimal" compression: the honest claim is only that nested rules compress better than one flat layer. Grammar-based compression algorithms such as Sequitur work by building nested rules.

If asked where the idea comes from: this is Herbert Simon's point from the compression slide's backup notes ("The Architecture of Complexity," 1962, p. 478): hierarchic systems are "usually composed of only a few different kinds of subsystems, in various combinations and arrangements."

Bridge to 4b, the second objection (no slide; this ends 4a. Credit Jason Brunson's GUGOG paper, "Balancing constriction and expansion for domain-specific grammars"): "Jason's paper argues that each domain needs a dialect, with its own objects and its own constraints on how they combine. GoTree is a dialect in that sense, with its own objects. His second half, the constraints, is where the objection comes in. The more you let people extend the language, the more it seems you give up safety."

His words, for reference: "From each specialty should be derived a graphical dialect, constituting both permitted objects and constraints on their combination." He proposes this as a middle ground between extensions that allow misleading graphics and a separate re-implementation for each domain.
