1 · Compression is the yardstick

the usage curve

Target: 0:20. Running total: 0:35 of 7:50.

  • This curve shows how often a chart is made, against how complex it is. It has a long tail. Bar, line, and scatter charts are on the left. One-lab infographics and diagrams are on the right.
  • The grammar of graphics covers the common charts on the left.
  • The long tail on the right is where people have built new grammars, e.g., for genomics, trees, and animation.
  • Name the two ends. Chart typologies like seaborn are on one end: a short call for each common chart, and a new function for every unusual one. Low-level toolkits like d3 are on the other end: a small vocabulary, and a long spec for every chart.
  • The curve is schematic, not measured.

Lead into the grammars picture: "Here are some of the grammars we have built for the tail."

The questions slide at the end asks whether this curve could become a benchmark.

Target: 0:15. Running total: 0:50 of 7:50.

  • One of the big ideas of the grammar of graphics was to write many different chart types in one shared language.
  • Because the charts shared one language, we could ask questions about the structure of charts, such as questions about marks and channels, and not only questions about chart types. For example, Draco and CompassQL both work on Vega-Lite specs, so they reason about a chart in terms of its marks and encodings.
  • For the long tail, we built many new grammars instead. Each one is its own language.
  • I wonder whether we have lost sight of what the original grammar of graphics gave us.

a good grammar is a good compression

Bar length: the size of the language, plus every spec written in it, weighted by how often each chart is made.

What are the obstacles to doing this compression?

Target: 0:25. Running total: 1:30 of 7:50.

  • One function per chart type gives a big language. Specs stay short, but the language grows with every chart in the tail.
  • Low-level primitives give a tiny language and long specs, even for the common charts.
  • A good grammar keeps both small. The total cost is the size of the language plus the size of every spec written in it, weighted by how often each chart is made. That is the yardstick for the rest of the talk.
  • The bar sizes are schematic, not measured.

The question for the talk (click for the line): "What are the obstacles to doing this compression?" The rest of the talk looks for them. Part 2 names the first one in the grammar of graphics, and part 4 takes three more in turn.
