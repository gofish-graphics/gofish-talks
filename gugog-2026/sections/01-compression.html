the usage curve

Target: 0:20. Running total: 0:35 of 7:35.

  • This curve shows how often a chart is made, against how complex it is. It has a long tail. Bar, line, and scatter charts are on the left. One-lab infographics and diagrams are on the right.
  • The grammar of graphics covers the common charts on the left.
  • The long tail on the right is where people have built new grammars, e.g., for genomics, trees, and animation.
  • Name the two ends. Chart typologies like seaborn are on one end: a short call for each common chart, and a new function for every unusual one. Low-level toolkits like d3 are on the other end: a small vocabulary, and a long spec for every chart.
  • The curve is schematic, not measured.

  • One of the big ideas of the grammar of graphics was to write many different chart types in one shared language.
  • Because the charts shared one language, we could ask questions about the structure of charts, such as questions about marks and channels, and not only questions about chart types. For example, Draco and CompassQL both work on Vega-Lite specs, so they reason about a chart in terms of its marks and encodings.
  • For the long tail, we built many new grammars instead. Each one is its own language.
  • I wonder whether we have lost sight of what the original grammar of graphics gave us.

1 · Compression is the yardstick

a grammar is a compression

What are the obstacles to doing this compression?

Target: 0:35. Running total: 1:40 of 7:35.

  • All three panels share one x axis: simple, common charts on the left and complex tail charts on the right. The left panel is the usage curve again. Most of the charts people make are the simple ones on the left, so the cost on the left counts most. The middle panel is how big the language has to be to reach those charts. The right panel is how long the spec for one of those charts is.
  • (Click.) The blue line is a chart typology like seaborn, with one function per chart type. Its specs stay short, so it is flat in the right panel. But it needs a new function for every chart in the tail, so it climbs steeply in the middle panel.
  • (Click.) The grey line is a low-level toolkit like d3. Its language is small and stays flat. But its specs are long even for a bar chart, and they get longer as the charts get more complex.
  • (Click.) The purple line is the grammar of graphics. It is excellent for the common charts, and those are most of what people make (point at the usage panel). Its language is small and its parts combine well, so its specs are short. Here it sits right next to the sweet spot.
  • Further out in the tail, for charts like ribbon, waffle, and mosaic charts, the usual way to write them tends to move toward one end or the other. The GoFish paper calls this an expressivity cliff. It says the grammar of graphics "presents some sharp expressivity cliffs." In practice the change is gradual, so I draw a bend and not a drop, and the line never reaches either end.
  • In ggplot2 the usual move is a new chart type. Extensions such as ggalluvial, ggmosaic, and ggwaffle each add one. So the size of the language bends up toward seaborn.
  • In Vega-Lite the usual move is to compute the layout with explicit transforms. You can see the start of this in Vega-Lite's own example gallery, in the wheat and wages chart and the waterfall chart. So the length of each spec bends up toward d3. Sometimes it goes the other way: the boxplot mark is closer to a chart type than to a primitive.
  • (Click.) The red line is the sweet spot. Its language is small and stops growing, and its specs stay short across the whole range. Every one of these designs is aiming for it. The total cost is the size of the language plus the length of every spec written in it, weighted by how often each chart is made. That is the yardstick for the rest of the talk.
  • The curves are schematic, not measured.

The question for the talk (click for the line): "What are the obstacles to doing this compression?" The rest of the talk looks for them. Part 2 names the first one in the grammar of graphics. Part 4 takes the central one head on, the choice between a big language and a small one that this chart shows, and then one objection to the answer.
