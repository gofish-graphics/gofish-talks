1 · Why compress

a grammar is a compression

What are the obstacles to doing this compression?

Target: 0:50 (was 0:35; the opening on why we compress takes about 0:15). Running total: 1:05 of 7:00.

Why compress (before the first click, over the empty axes):

  • Why unify charts into one language at all? The old reasons were that one language is easier to write and easier to read. Those matter less now that AI writes a lot of our code.
  • The reason that still holds is compression. Compressing forces you to find structure.
  • The grammar of graphics did exactly this. It compressed many chart types into one shared language, and that revealed structure like marks, channels, scales, and coordinate systems.
  • That structure is worth having for its own sake, the way structure in mathematics is. It also shaped the tools we use: ggplot2, Vega-Lite and Altair, and Tableau.
  • So how well do today's approaches compress?

If asked for an example of what the structure gives us: Draco and CompassQL work on Vega-Lite specs, so they reason about a chart in terms of its marks and encodings, not its chart type.

The chart:

  • All three panels share one x axis: simple, common charts on the left and complex tail charts on the right. The left panel is how often each chart gets made. It has a long tail: bar, line, and scatter charts are on the left, and one-lab infographics and diagrams are on the right. Most of the charts people make are the simple ones, so the cost on the left counts most. The middle panel is how big the language has to be to reach those charts. The right panel is how long the spec for one of those charts is.
  • (Click.) The blue line is a chart typology like seaborn, with one function per chart type. Its specs stay short, so it is flat in the right panel. But it needs a new function for every chart in the tail, so it climbs steeply in the middle panel. Its language is huge.
  • (Click.) The grey line is a low-level toolkit like d3. Its language is small and stays flat. But its specs are long even for a bar chart, and they get longer as the charts get more complex.
  • (Click.) The purple line is the grammar of graphics. It is excellent for the common charts, and those are most of what people make (point at the usage panel). Its language is small and its parts combine well, so its specs are short. Here it sits right next to the sweet spot.
  • Further out in the tail, for charts like ribbon, waffle, and mosaic charts, the usual way to write them tends to move toward one end or the other. The GoFish paper calls this an expressivity cliff. It says the grammar of graphics "presents some sharp expressivity cliffs." In practice the change is gradual, so I draw a bend and not a drop, and the line never reaches either end.
  • In ggplot2 the usual move is a new chart type. Extensions such as ggalluvial, ggmosaic, and ggwaffle each add one. So the size of the language bends up toward seaborn.
  • In Vega-Lite the usual move is to compute the layout with explicit transforms. You can see the start of this in Vega-Lite's own example gallery, in the wheat and wages chart and the waterfall chart. So the length of each spec bends up toward d3. Sometimes it goes the other way: the boxplot mark is closer to a chart type than to a primitive.
  • (Click.) The red line is the sweet spot. Its language is small and stops growing, and its specs stay short across the whole range. Every one of these designs is aiming for it. The total cost is the size of the language plus the length of every spec written in it, weighted by how often each chart is made. That is the yardstick for the rest of the talk.
  • The curves are schematic, not measured. The questions slide at the end asks whether the usage curve could become a benchmark.

The question for the talk (click for the line): "What are the obstacles to doing this compression?" The first one is the cliff itself.

  • (Click.) Seaborn needs a new function for every new chart, so it is high and flat in the middle. Its specs stay short, so it sits at zero on the right.
  • (Click.) d3 adds no new words, so it sits at zero in the middle. Its specs keep getting longer, so it is high on the right.
  • (Click.) The grammar of graphics pays a little at the start for its core, and then almost nothing for the common charts. At the expressivity cliff its cost starts to climb.
