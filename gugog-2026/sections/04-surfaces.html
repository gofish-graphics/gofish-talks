4a · Many surfaces against one language

… we suggest that consideration of small modular language components may be helpful in the continuation and extension of this ecosystem. There are many tasks, and no one DSL will be able to capture all of them without compromising essential parts of its domain design. That is, there is no grammar to rule them all.

Andrew McNutt, No Grammar to Rule Them All, IEEE VIS 2022

Full passage (reference markers removed): Park et al. argue that efforts should be made to “find a definitive grammar that can unify many of these existing grammars”. However, Greenspun’s tenth rule quips that any sufficiently complicated program contains an ad hoc, informally specified, bug-ridden, slow implementation of half of Lisp. Less satirically Fowler notes that one of the biggest dangers in DSL design is “Sliding into generality.” While tools like Vega-Lite are probably not in danger of becoming Lisp, we suggest that consideration of small modular language components may be helpful in the continuation and extension of this ecosystem. There are many tasks, and no one DSL will be able to capture all of them without compromising essential parts of its domain design. That is, there is no grammar to rule them all.

Target: 0:15 (shown, not recited; read the accent phrase and the last sentence aloud). Running total: 3:45 of 7:35.

The obstacle, in its strongest form: a field has many tasks, so it needs many API surfaces, and one language that tries to cover them all compromises each domain's design. A second angle is that, even with many surfaces, it is not clear they can be tied together in one language.

Say: "Andrew's survey concluded there's no grammar to rule them all, and I agree that no single surface should do everything. His suggestion is small modular language components, and that is the direction GoFish takes. GoTree is an example: it keeps its own domain design and compiles to a shared core." Then go to the next slide.

This is his idea, credited, and the next slides build on it. The same paper argues for "compile towers": "Compilations may be chained together in a compile tower in a manner supported by targeted languages that do one thing well (akin to the Unix credo)", and "As the ecosystem of JSON-style DSLs continues to grow it may be advantageous to select designs that re-use as much work of the previous DSLs as possible." It even suggests that "DSLs like Atom may be well matched with a compile tower-style strategy". GoTree on GoFish is one such tower.

The quote is verbatim from Andrew McNutt, "No Grammar to Rule Them All: A Survey of JSON-style DSLs for Visualization", IEEE VIS 2022 (arXiv:2207.07998), except that its reference markers ([82] after "Park et al.", [29] after "tenth rule", [24] after "Fowler") were removed. The excerpt keeps the paper's order and opens with an ellipsis for "While tools like Vega-Lite are probably not in danger of becoming Lisp,". The accent color on "small modular language components" is ours.

  • Many surfaces against one language. Domain grammars can be layered on a shared core instead of being separate languages.

The obstacle: it can look like you have to choose between one universal grammar and many domain-specific ones. Andrew McNutt's survey (the quote two slides back) already points the way out: small modular language components, and domain languages that compile to ones that do one thing well. This slide is one instance of that idea, credited to him, not an argument with him.

GoFish is not trying to be the single definitive grammar that Park et al. call for (the call McNutt pushes back on). It is a shared core that domain grammars can build on, which is consistent with McNutt's point.

Say (v3 draft's framing): "Domain grammars need words and syntax that fit their tasks. That rules out one surface interface for all visualization work. It does not rule out one language system." Here's an example of the modular approach Andrew described. Same tree as the last slide; now here's how it's written. On the left is GoTree's own spec, in its own JSON language. On the right is GoTree rebuilt as a library on GoFish. It keeps GoTree's tree words: a node, a link, a parent-child rule, a sibling rule. And each of GoTree's relations is a GoFish verb: within and align are align, juxtapose and flatten are distribute.

The render is on the previous slide; this slide shows only the two specs, so they get the room.

Sources: code and render are the final GUGOG paper's Figure 5 (section 3.3 "GoTree"), from examples/gotree-node-link.json, examples/gofish-gotree-node-link.ts and figures/gofish-gotree-node-link.svg, copied exactly. The paper's role letters: A node = circle, B link = line, C within = x align middle, D flatten = x distribute, E juxtapose = y distribute, F align top = y align start. Say: And here's how it's layered. The combine that the tree spec called turns each axis into an ordinary GoFish constraint, Constraint.align or Constraint.distribute, inside a layer(...).relate(...), the same core form from the intro slide. So GoTree keeps its own tree words, and underneath it is plain GoFish: same renderer, same constraints. The paper: "we get support for coordinate transforms, scales, and custom marks without having to design them from scratch." Domain grammars can be layered on a shared core instead of being separate languages: Andrew's compile tower, with GoFish as one floor of it. They keep their own design and share the core.

v3 draft wording, if useful: "The compiler turns this program into ordinary GoFish structure, so GoTree can use the GoFish graphics pipeline and renderer. Its alignment and containment relations also use the same constraints as other GoFish programs. Researchers can therefore use these shared terms to state what GoTree adds, even though GoTree keeps its own tree interface."

Bridge to 4b (no slide; credit Jason Brunson's GUGOG paper, "Balancing constriction and expansion for domain-specific grammars"): "Jason's paper puts these two obstacles together. He argues that each domain needs a dialect, with its own objects and its own constraints on how they combine. GoTree is a dialect. The next question is about the constraints."

His words, for reference: "From each specialty should be derived a graphical dialect, constituting both permitted objects and constraints on their combination." He proposes this as a middle ground between extensions that allow misleading graphics and a separate re-implementation for each domain. Keep the reference at this level in the talk; his geometric data analysis case study is not needed here.
