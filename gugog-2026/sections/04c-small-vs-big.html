4c · A small language against a big one

I should not design a small language, and I should not design a large one. I need to design a language that can grow.

Guy L. Steele Jr., Growing a Language, OOPSLA 1998

Target: 0:15. Running total: 6:20 of 7:35.

  • Tie it to part 1. A small core keeps the language small, and growing from within gives the big vocabulary that short specs need.
  • A small core and an extensible core do not conflict. An extensible core can be small, as long as people extend it from within the language. Growing from within is Steele's answer to the choice between small and big.

Source: Guy L. Steele Jr., "Growing a Language", keynote at OOPSLA 1998, published in Higher-Order and Symbolic Computation 12(3), 1999. 4c · A small language against a big one

a language that grows is a tree

Build new structures from old ones with a small, stable set of verbs.

Target: 0:10. Running total: 6:30 of 7:35.

  • Open with the callback: "Earlier I asked whether we keep adding nouns forever. Here is the answer."
  • This is what a language that can grow looks like. On the left is the flat layer from part 2. On the right, the same charts sit on intermediate words (chart, tree, diagram), and those words are built from a small core of marks and verbs like spread, stack, and relate.
  • The middle nodes are domain languages. Chart, tree, and diagram each have their own words, fit to their own domain, and they are all written in the same language on the same core. So you can have a custom language for each subdomain and still have one integrated language. GoTree, which comes back from 4a, is one of these.
  • This answers the question from the part 2 slide. We do not have to keep adding nouns to the core. New words are built from old ones, so the core stays small while the vocabulary gets big. That is the compression picture from the opening.
  • Say the thesis: "In the traditional grammar of graphics, the only way to grow the language is to add a new primitive. In a language with verbs, you can build new structures from old ones, using a fairly stable set of composition mechanisms."
  • The faded node in the middle is the point: the middle is not a closed set of three kinds. New levels, and other grammars like GoTree, can keep being added.

The leaves are charts the audience has already seen. The population pyramid, GoTree silhouette tree, Sankey tree, and Bluefish pulley diagram are on the splash wall. The ribbon chart is from the crash course.

If asked about "optimal" compression: the honest claim is only that nested rules compress better than one flat layer. Grammar-based compression algorithms such as Sequitur work by building nested rules.

4c · A small language against a big one

where we are now

so far

  • Charts grow from within.
  • Marks reduce to simple primitives.
  • Interfaces reduce to bounding boxes and boundary curves.

not yet

  • Our verbs are a typology of operators.

The next cliff: a layout language more expressive than a fixed set of operators.

Target: 0:25. Running total: 6:55 of 7:35.

"We replaced a typology of charts with a grammar. But our verbs, like spread, stack, and relate, are themselves a typology, a typology of operators. When you need a layout that is not in the set, you hit the next cliff. Today you add it from outside the language, which is the same add-a-new-primitive move from part 2."

"I think the way past it is a layout or constraint language that is more expressive than what we have now. That is the question I would most like help with."
