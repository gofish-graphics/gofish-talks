4a · A big language or a small one

I should not design a small language, and I should not design a large one. I need to design a language that can grow.

Guy L. Steele Jr., Growing a Language, OOPSLA 1998

Target: 0:15. Running total: 3:45 of 7:35.

This is the tradeoff from the opening chart, said directly. It is the one central tradeoff of the talk.

  • A big language, like seaborn, covers many charts with short specs, but it needs a new function for every new chart.
  • A small language, like d3, stays small, but every spec is long.
  • Steele says not to pick either one. The middle path is a hierarchy of abstractions: a small core, and new levels built from the ones below it. The small core keeps the language small, and the levels give the big vocabulary that short specs need.

Say: "Back to the compression chart. It looks like you have to choose. "The middle path is a hierarchy of abstractions: a small core, with new levels built on top of it."

Target: 0:10. Running total: 4:10 of 7:35.

  • Open with the callback: "Earlier I asked whether we keep adding nouns forever. Here is the answer."
  • This is what a language that can grow looks like. On the left is the flat layer from part 2. On the right, the same charts sit on intermediate words (chart, tree, diagram), and those words are built from a small core of marks and verbs like spread, stack, and relate.
  • The middle nodes are domain languages, Andrew's small modular components. Chart, tree, and diagram each have their own words, fit to their own domain.
  • Extensibility is what keeps the tree together. Each new DSL is built from the same core with the same verbs, from inside the language. So the DSLs do not fall apart into separate languages, the way the grammars in the opening tangle did. You get a custom language for each subdomain and still have one language. GoTree, on the next slides, is one of these branches.
  • This answers the question from the part 2 slide. We do not have to keep adding nouns to the core. New words are built from old ones, so the core stays small while the vocabulary gets big. That is the compression picture from the opening.
  • Say the thesis: "In the traditional grammar of graphics, the only way to grow the language is to add a new primitive. In a language with verbs, you can build new structures from old ones, using a fairly stable set of composition mechanisms."
  • The faded node in the middle is the point: the middle is not a closed set of three kinds. New levels, and other grammars like GoTree, can keep being added.

Credit Jason Brunson's GUGOG paper, "Balancing constriction and expansion for domain-specific grammars"): "Jason's paper argues that each domain needs a dialect, with its own objects and its own constraints on how they combine. GoTree is a dialect in that sense, with its own objects. His second half, the constraints, is where the objection comes in. The more you let people extend the language, the more it seems you give up safety."

His words, for reference: "From each specialty should be derived a graphical dialect, constituting both permitted objects and constraints on their combination." He proposes this as a middle ground between extensions that allow misleading graphics and a separate re-implementation for each domain.
