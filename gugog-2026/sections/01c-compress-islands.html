This slide and the next show the evidence before the verbs slide names the pattern. The ideas are Atom's and PiCCL's, and the credit goes to their authors.

Say: Here's Atom, by Park, Drucker, Fernandez, and Elmqvist. It is a grammar for unit visualizations, where one mark stands for one thing, like one passenger on the Titanic. Its marks are plain circles and rectangles. What Atom added is a small set of layout operators, like FillX and MaxFill. They split a space into cells and arrange the units inside them, and you nest them to place every unit.

Source: Park, Drucker, Fernandez and Elmqvist, "ATOM: A Grammar for Unit Visualizations", IEEE TVCG 2018. The top figure is the paper's teaser and the bottom one is its layout operator figure. PiCCL: Data-Driven Composition of Bespoke Pictorial Charts · Shi et al., IEEE VIS 2025

Target: 0:10. Running total: 2:00 of 7:00.

Say: And here's PiCCL, by Shi and others at VIS 2025, a grammar for pictorial charts like these. Its marks are ordinary pictures and shapes. What PiCCL added is a set of operators for repeating pictures and combining them, e.g., "A atop B" paints one shape onto another and keeps only the part inside it.

Lead into the verbs slide: "So neither island mainly added a new kind of mark or channel. Each one added new ways to arrange and combine things. I think most of the other islands are like this too. They add ways to arrange, combine, repeat, or connect marks."

Source: Shi et al., "PiCCL: Data-Driven Composition of Bespoke Pictorial Charts", IEEE VIS 2025. The left figure is the paper's teaser and the right one is its compositing operator figure. Both are copied from the thesis defense deck's PiCCL intro slide, whose notes say the same thing, that the core idea of the paper is this set of compositing operators. The lead line follows the GUGOG paper's abstract, which reads recent grammars as extensions of the grammar of graphics "with verbs for arranging, compositing, repeating, or otherwise relating marks to each other".
