Say (the bridge): "What if one language held all of these? You could do this without unifying anything: one package that bundles every grammar as a separate component. That would fix installing them. It would not let you mix them. Each island's ideas would still only work inside that island, so you could not use Atom's unit layout inside a PiCCL picture, or put a GoTree layout inside a chart. Mixing needs something the islands have in common. So is there anything in common? Look at what each island added."

Why this beat is here: the user's worry was that the circle on its own does not justify unifying anything, because a bundle of unrelated modular components would also put everything in one place. The bridge grants that, and says what a bundle cannot do, which is mixing. Mixing is the reason to look for shared structure, and the next three slides look for it.

Fairness: building a separate grammar was the right move for each of these teams. Each one was getting past the cliff with the tools that existed. Nothing here says they should have done it differently. The point is only that today their ideas cannot be mixed, and that is a problem we all share.

This slide and the next two (PiCCL and GoTree) show the evidence before the verbs slide names the pattern. The ideas are Atom's, PiCCL's, and GoTree's, and the credit goes to their authors.

Say: Here's Atom, by Park, Drucker, Fernandez, and Elmqvist. It is a grammar for unit visualizations, where one mark stands for one thing, like one passenger on the Titanic. Its marks are plain circles and rectangles. What Atom added is a small set of layout operators, like FillX and MaxFill. They split a space into cells and arrange the units inside them, and you nest them to place every unit.

GoTree: A Grammar of Tree Visualizations · Li, Tian, Xu, McGuffin & Yuan, ACM CHI 2020

Say: And here's GoTree, by Guozheng Li, Min Tian, Qinmei Xu, Michael McGuffin, and Xiaoru Yuan, at CHI 2020. It is a grammar for tree visualizations, like all of these. Its marks are plain nodes and links. What GoTree added is a small set of layout operators. On each axis, a parent and its children can be placed by include, juxtapose, or within, and siblings can be aligned or flattened. Mix those per axis and you get all of these layouts.

Lead into the verbs slide: "So none of these three islands mainly added a new kind of mark or channel. Each one added new ways to arrange and combine things. I think most of the other islands are like this too. They add ways to arrange, combine, repeat, or connect marks."

GoTree is also the island whose data is not a table. It comes back on the "same core, three shapes of data" slide in part 4a, rebuilt as a library on GoFish. The GoTree code slides in 4a are hidden for now, so that slide is the only place its GoFish version appears.
