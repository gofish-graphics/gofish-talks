The story: the talk opens on the hook, over the islands drawing: I love visualization grammars, and I'm sad, because I can't use them together. They are different systems that don't work with each other, and a lot of good work has no clear path into practice. Why are there so many islands? The compression chart answers it. The grammar of graphics compresses the common charts very well, then hits the expressivity cliff, and each island is someone getting past the cliff on their own. So what if we compress the islands into one language? A bundle of separate grammars would hold them all but would not let you mix them, so we need what they have in common. To make a description shorter, you have to find the structure (Simon, 1962), and a language with only nouns finds nouns. Atom, PiCCL, and GoTree each added operators, so the grammar of graphics needs verbs. A wall of GoFish charts names GoFish, which adds them. The three grammars' examples introduce it, one idea each: Atom shows operators in a chart's flow, PiCCL shows operators on marks and then relations, and GoTree's layouts are written with the same relations. A fair objection follows. Andrew McNutt's is that one language cannot serve every domain's design; with verbs, a DSL is a library built inside the language, as GoTree shows. The conclusion says that the grammar of graphics needs verbs. Our verbs are themselves a typology of operators, and that is the next cliff and the first question for the panel. In the bonus slides after the panel, Cynthia Huang and Matthew Kay's objection is that growth costs safety, and a small type system brings it back, as CanSum shows.
