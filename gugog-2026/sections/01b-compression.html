Why compress (before the first click, over the empty axes; the line under the title is on screen from the start):

  • Why unify charts into one language at all? The old reasons were that one language is easier to write and easier to read. Those matter less now that AI writes a lot of our code.
  • The reason that still holds is that compression forces us to find structure. To make a description shorter, you have to find the structure in what you describe.
  • The grammar of graphics is an example. It compressed many chart types into marks, channels, scales, and coordinates. That structure is worth finding for its own sake, and it shaped the tools we use: ggplot2, Vega-Lite and Altair, and Tableau.
  • Herbert Simon made this point in 1962. Most complex things are full of repetition, and we can use that to describe them simply. But he adds a condition: we have to find the right representation.
  • That condition matters for this talk. Which structure you find depends on the language you describe charts in. A language with only nouns finds nouns.
  • So how well do today's approaches compress?

If asked for an example of what the structure gives us: Draco and CompassQL work on Vega-Lite specs, so they reason about a chart in terms of its marks and encodings, not its chart type.

If asked where "compression begets structure" comes from:

  • Simon's own words, from the summary of "The Architecture of Complexity" (Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 106(6), 1962, p. 481): "How complex or simple a structure is depends critically upon the way in which we describe it. Most of the complex structures found in the world are enormously redundant, and we can use this redundancy to simplify their description. But to use it, to achieve the simplification, we must find the right representation."
  • The formal version is the minimum description length principle: compressing is learning the regularities. Peter Grünwald's tutorial (2004, arXiv math/0406077, section 1.1): "any regularity in the data can be used to compress the data, i.e. to describe it using fewer symbols than the number of symbols needed to describe the data literally. The more regularities there are, the more the data can be compressed. Equating 'learning' with 'finding regularity', we can therefore say that the more we are able to compress the data, the more we have learned about the data."
  • Simon also says that hierarchy is what makes complex things describable in few words (p. 478): "The hierarchic structures we have been discussing have a high degree of redundancy, hence can often be described in economical terms." The first kind of redundancy he names is that "Hierarchic systems are usually composed of only a few different kinds of subsystems, in various combinations and arrangements." And he warns (p. 477): "If there are important systems in the world that are complex without being hierarchic, they may to a considerable extent escape our observation and our understanding." This is the same idea as the hierarchy slide in 4a.
  • An honest caveat: compression does not always give structure that people can read. The shortest description of something can be opaque. Simon's point is that nesting a few kinds of reusable parts is what makes a short description readable, and that is the thesis of this talk.
  • Gregory Chaitin says it in one line (2002, arXiv math/0210035): "comprehension is compression, i.e., explaining many facts using few theoretical assumptions".
  • An example from machine learning, grokking (not checked against the papers; verify before saying it): small networks trained with weight decay on modular addition first memorize the training data, and later switch to a compact algorithm with clear structure (Power et al. 2022; Nanda et al. 2023).

The chart:

  • All three panels share one x axis: simple, common charts on the left and complex tail charts on the right. The left panel is how often each chart gets made. It has a long tail: bar, line, and scatter charts are on the left, and one-lab infographics and diagrams are on the right. Most of the charts people make are the simple ones, so the cost on the left counts most. The middle panel is how big the language has to be to reach those charts. The right panel is how long the spec for one of those charts is.
  • (Click.) The blue line is a chart typology like seaborn, with one function per chart type. Its specs stay short, so it is flat in the right panel. But it needs a new function for every chart in the tail, so it climbs steeply in the middle panel. Its language is huge.
  • (Click.) The grey line is a low-level toolkit like d3. Its language is small and stays flat. But its specs are long even for a bar chart, and they get longer as the charts get more complex.
  • (Click.) The purple line is the grammar of graphics. It is excellent for the common charts, and those are most of what people make (point at the usage panel). Its language is small and its parts combine well, so its specs are short. Here it sits right next to the sweet spot.
  • Further out in the tail, for charts like ribbon, waffle, and mosaic charts, the usual way to write them tends to move toward one end or the other. The GoFish paper calls this an expressivity cliff. It says the grammar of graphics "presents some sharp expressivity cliffs." In practice the change is gradual, so I draw a bend and not a drop, and the line never reaches either end.
  • In ggplot2 the usual move is a new chart type. Extensions such as ggalluvial, ggmosaic, and ggwaffle each add one. So the size of the language bends up toward seaborn.
  • In Vega-Lite the usual move is to compute the layout with explicit transforms. You can see the start of this in Vega-Lite's own example gallery, in the wheat and wages chart and the waterfall chart. So the length of each spec bends up toward d3. Sometimes it goes the other way: the boxplot mark is closer to a chart type than to a primitive.
  • (Click.) The red line is the sweet spot. Its language is small and stops growing, and its specs stay short across the whole range. Every one of these designs is aiming for it. The total cost is the size of the language plus the length of every spec written in it, weighted by how often each chart is made. That is the yardstick for the rest of the talk.
  • The curves are schematic, not measured. The questions slide at the end asks whether the usage curve could become a benchmark.

The question for the talk (click for the line): "What are the obstacles to doing this compression?" The first one is the cliff itself.
